# Copywriting

Good copy makes the reader feel understood and tells them exactly what they get. This skill writes page and interface copy that leads with the outcome, uses the reader's own words, and keeps insider jargon out.

Pair it with `dejan-voice` when the copy goes out under Dejan's name, and with `ux-designer` and `ui-designer` for structure and layout.

## House rules (always on)

- No em dashes and no en dashes. Use periods, commas or colons.
- US spelling.
- No apology words in copy ("sorry", "unfortunately", "we apologize").
- Plain words over clever ones. If a newcomer would ask "what does that mean?", rewrite it.
- No invented numbers, testimonials or logos. If proof is missing, leave a marked gap: `[PROOF NEEDED: ...]`.
- Prices, dates and formats come from the current source of truth (the live offer page, the site's offers file or the Maven listing). Never from memory.

## Step 1: Context (ask once, only for what is missing)

If the repo or workspace has a product marketing context file, read it first.

1. **Page and goal.** Course page, Maven listing, home page, workshop one-pager, pricing, email capture, in-product screen? What is the single action you want?
2. **Reader.** Who exactly (for example: early PMs, designers, CX and ops people who use AI for emails but have never built anything; or a team lead buying for 10 people)? What are they stuck on today? What worries them about buying? Which words do they use for their goal?
3. **Offer.** What is it, what does someone leave with, how is it different from the alternatives they are weighing (self study, other cohort courses, internal training)? What proof exists?
4. **Arrival.** Where does the traffic come from (LinkedIn post, masterclass, email, search, Maven browse)? What do they already know?

## Step 2: The three answers

Before any section copy, write these three lines. Everything else on the page supports them.

1. **What we are going to do.** The concrete result, in the reader's words. Example shape: "In two weeks you turn a real problem from your job into a working first version you can share with a link."
2. **How we will do it.** Format and rhythm in one or two lines. Example shape: "Four live sessions of two hours. Each one ends with something finished."
3. **What you will do.** The reader's effort and what they walk away with. Example shape: "About an hour between sessions. You leave with a working first version, test results from real users and a 30 day plan."

If you cannot write these three cleanly, the offer is not clear yet. Say so instead of covering it with adjectives.

## Step 3: Principles

- **Clear beats clever.** Busy readers skim.
- **Outcome before contents.** "Ship a working first version in two weeks" beats "four sessions with live demos".
- **Benefit, then feature.** Say what the feature means for their week or career.
- **Specific beats vague.** "Two hours, twice a week, for two weeks" beats "flexible schedule".
- **Their words, not ours.** Mirror how the audience talks in posts, comments and calls. Translate internal terms ("eval", "context", "skill file") into plain language the first time, or drop them.
- **One idea per section.** Each section moves one argument forward.

## Step 4: Line by line style

1. Short common words ("use", "help", "start").
2. Active voice ("You build", not "is built").
3. Confident: cut "very", "really", "almost", "just".
4. Show the result instead of piling on adverbs.
5. Honest: no hype, no fake urgency. Scarcity only when real (seat cap, closing date).

Quick review: any jargon an outsider would trip on? Any sentence doing two jobs? Passive voice? Exclamation marks (cut almost all)? Buzzwords with nothing behind them?

## Step 5: Page patterns

**Course page or Maven listing** (in this order):
1. Outcome headline.
2. Who it is for, and who it is not for.
3. What you will do: sessions written as "You leave with ..." lines.
4. Proof: past learners, results, the instructor's experience. Real only.
5. How it works: dates, live hours, time between sessions, group size, tools needed.
6. Objections and FAQ: time, price, "I am not technical", "I am not a PM", company reimbursement.
7. Final call to action, with whatever risk reversal genuinely exists.

**Home page or personal site:** say who you help and with what in one line, offer clear paths by visitor type (individual, team, leader), and put credibility near the top.

**Workshop or team one-pager:** speak to the buyer (team outcome, time, cost, invoice) and to the participant (what they will build) separately.

**In-product screens** (learning platforms, dashboards): orient and encourage. "Continue where you stopped", "Next up for you". Progress messages feel supportive, not clinical. Blockers (payment, missing info) are clear and blame free. Empty states invite action. Keep labels under about 12 words.

## Step 6: Calls to action

Avoid generic labels such as Get started, Learn more, Submit, Click here or Sign up.
Use: verb + what they get + qualifier. Examples: "Save my seat", "See the four sessions", "Book a free intro call", "Join the next cohort", "Get the free masterclass", "Continue lesson 2".

## Step 7: Above the fold

- **Headline:** the change the reader wants, specific and outcome led.
- **Subhead:** one concrete detail that makes the headline believable, one or two sentences.
- **Primary CTA:** the one action.

Then the supporting sections: proof, the problem in their words, three to five benefits, how it works in three or four steps, objections, final CTA.

## Output

1. **Copy by section:** headline, subhead, CTA, then each section's header and body, then secondary CTAs.
2. **Notes** on key choices: what principle it applies and why.
3. **Options:** two or three alternatives for each headline and CTA, each with a one line reason.
4. **Meta (when relevant):** SEO title (50 to 60 characters), meta description (150 to 160), Open Graph title (up to about 60) and description (about 150 to 200).
5. **Jargon swaps:** a short table of insider terms you replaced and what you used instead.
